The Stranger Within

NATİQ MİSİРZADƏ · English

In the old streets of Istanbul, Kerem, a bookseller who has withdrawn from his past into a solitude of his own, is drawn into a past he cannot explain after an ordinary encounter with Elif. She claims to have known him for years, gradually bringing out childhood memories, shared experiences, and photographs. Yet nothing Kerem remembers matches what Elif tells him. Can a person forget their own past, or is the greatest stranger sometimes the self?

THE STRANGER WITHIN

Night had settled over the room like a weight that slowed not only the passage of time but the inner world of a human being as well. The sounds of Istanbul outside seemed to have fallen several floors below; it was as though this room existed not in another city, but inside another mind, cutting off every thread that connected it to the continuity of the outside world. As I sat at the desk, moving through the pages of an open notebook, I felt that I was not reading a text so much as the way of thinking someone had left behind.

The pages were unfinished. Sentences did not reach their endings; some broke off in the very middle of a thought, while others continued in a disorderly flow, as if they had been forced not to be written but to spill out from within. Even that disorder carried a strange sense of order, because some lives are left behind not to be told neatly, but to be understood in fragments.

The vibration of the phone broke the silence. I answered without looking at the screen. The voice on the other end sounded more familiar this time—not angry, not calm, but tired, worn down by the same question being asked again and again over a long period of time.

“Emrah. When are you coming home? Are you staying at work again?” the voice asked.

I let a brief silence pass. I looked at the notebook, then at the darkness beyond the window.

“I have work,” I said, my voice low but firm.

“I’ll be late.”

There was a moment's hesitation on the other end. Nothing was said in that gap, yet the weight of what remained unspoken was unmistakable.

Then the voice returned, softer now, coming from somewhere more personal:

“The child keeps asking for you…”

A breath.

“He keeps asking when his father is coming home…”

The sentence did not fall into the room; it landed directly in my mind. But the voice continued.

“He wants to play chess with you… He sits at the table and sets up the pieces, but never starts… He waits… Sometimes he falls asleep while he’s waiting…”

Another brief pause.

“If you’re going to be much later, tell me… at least we won’t keep him waiting.”

It was not an accusation. It was an acceptance.

As though this waiting was no longer anything new, but simply part of a routine that had been going on for a long time. I did not answer for a while. Some answers are not given; they are merely postponed. Then I drew a short breath.

“All right,” I said.

And I hung up. When I put the phone back on the desk, its screen went dark, but the silence in the room did not return to what it had been. The trace of a conversation remained there now, carrying something heavier than the words that had been spoken.

I returned to the notebook.

Turned the page.

And continued reading.

But what I was reading was no longer merely writing. It was the slow unraveling of a life from within itself. I still did not know whose life it was.

Istanbul is less a city in which a person lives than a consciousness that settles inside the human mind and keeps moving there—never falling completely silent, yet never speaking plainly either. Every day spent here therefore becomes a quiet journey, not so much through the outer world as through the layers of one's inner world. Kerem had long been like a traveller lost somewhere within himself, without even realizing he was on such a journey. He was no longer someone who followed the reality outside him; he had become someone accustomed to leaving the emptiness within himself unfilled.
